## The argument

Agriculture is where technology confronts physical reality. Soil variability, weather that does not read the release notes, aging equipment, thin margins, and labour markets that do not respond to software updates. The farm cannot be A/B tested. It cannot be rolled back. Failure is measured in seasons, not sprints. That makes farming the most honest test of what AI can and cannot optimize, and it makes the lessons from agricultural adoption directly transferable to every other domain where the gap between a demo and a deployment is measured in stakes.

The pressures arrive together: AI adoption, climate disruption, trade fragmentation, labour shortages, and rural depopulation. They are not separate problems. They are interconnected stresses on a system that policy, technology deployment, and most leadership frameworks still treat as background. Food is not a sector adjacent to the big questions. It is where they become visible.

The through-line I keep returning to is ownership. As farms become programmable, the software layer decides who captures the value and who is left renting access to their own operation. Open source is not an ideological preference in that argument. It is the practical difference between a tool you own and a subscription you depend on, and it is the difference between automation that increases a farm's autonomy and automation that quietly transfers it.

## The book

*The Programmable Farm* is in progress. It argues that open source, AI, climate pressure, and geopolitical turbulence are making the farm programmable, and that the stake is whether anyone who wants to farm still can. [Read the argument as it stands](https://thefutureherd.ca/knowledge/the-programmable-farm/).

## Who this is for

Agricultural associations, agri-food industry leaders, rural policymakers, food retailers and distributors, supply chain executives, rural development organizations, and investors with exposure to agricultural risk. Also general audiences who want AI literacy grounded in physical reality rather than optimism.

## What it looks like in the room

**Keynote.** What is actually changing on the ground, where the hype is most operationally dangerous, and what resilient automation looks like when it has to work in the mud.

**Policy briefing.** For public servants and elected officials who need the difference between a technology story and a structural one.

**Roundtable or salon.** Facilitated conversation across sectors that normally talk past each other, designed for emergence rather than theatre.

**Research.** Scoped briefings on specific questions: adoption, ownership, procurement, or sector risk.
