## The argument

Most organizations are being pushed into AI decisions without the literacy to know what they are actually buying, building, or delegating. That gap produces waste, reputational damage, and governance failures that were avoidable from the start.

The sharper question is not what AI can do. It is which decisions stay human. Systems have moved from answering questions to taking action: planning, drafting, communicating, deciding, executing. Delegation is now an operating decision with legal and ethical weight attached, and most institutions are making it by default rather than by design.

My position is a sequence. Literacy first: what the system is, where it creates value, where it creates dependency. Then delegation: which decisions can leave the room, under what thresholds, with what reversibility. Then oversight: who is accountable when it fails, and how anyone would know. Institutions that map decision authority before deployment keep the ability to govern. Those that adopt first and ask later inherit whatever defaults their vendors set for them.

## Who this is for

Executives and boards, legal and compliance teams, policy and program leads, technology leads, and any public-facing institution where automated decisions land on real people. Regulated sectors especially, where accountability cannot be delegated to a supplier.

## What it looks like in the room

**Keynote.** What is actually changing, what is marketing, and which decisions are being quietly moved. Built for a mixed room.

**Workshop.** Decision mapping against consequence, reversibility, and required judgment. Teams leave with a working inventory of what they have delegated and what they have not decided yet.

**Executive briefing.** A single session for a board or leadership team facing a specific deployment or a governance question that has already gone sideways.

**Advisory.** Governance frameworks written against your systems: acceptable use, oversight structures, accountability chains, escalation. Built to hold when systems are under pressure, not only when they work as intended.
